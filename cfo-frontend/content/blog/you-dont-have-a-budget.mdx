# The Excuse That Never Dies

It's March. Q1 results are in. Marketing is 40% over budget.

You sit down with the VP of Marketing to understand what happened. And then you hear it—the excuse that every finance leader knows by heart:

"Finance gave me that number."

Not "I committed to that number." Not "I own that number." Finance *gave* it to them. As if the budget was imposed from above, like weather. Something that happened to them, not something they agreed to.

You've heard this before. Maybe dozens of times. And every time, you know the real problem isn't the overspend. It's that nobody actually committed to anything in the first place.

---

## The Document vs. The Commitment

Every company has a budget. There's a file somewhere—Excel, Google Sheets, maybe even a planning tool. It went through rounds of review. Leadership approved it. The board saw a summary.

But having a document isn't the same as having commitment.

Here's a simple test: pick any line in your budget and ask "who signed for this?" Not who submitted the number. Not who was in the meeting when it was discussed. Who actually put their name on it and said "I commit to delivering this"?

In most companies, the answer is nobody. The budget exists, but nobody owns it. And when nobody owns it, nobody feels accountable when it breaks.

---

## The Three Gaps

There's usually not one problem with budget accountability. There are three, and they show up in a predictable pattern.

**The timing gap.** The fiscal year starts January 1st. When was the budget actually approved? In a lot of companies, "approved" happens in late January or February. Sometimes March. Which means Q1 is already running without a baseline. You're measuring performance against a number that didn't exist when the performance happened.

You've felt this. The awkward Q1 review where everyone knows the budget wasn't really final yet. The variance explanations that start with "well, the budget assumed..."

**The ownership gap.** Somewhere in your budget there's a line for "Software" or "Professional Services" or "Travel." Who owns it? Not which cost center it rolls up to. Which human being is responsible for staying within that number?

When the answer is vague—"well, it's shared across teams" or "Finance manages that centrally"—you've found a line that will overspend. Shared ownership is no ownership.

**The commitment gap.** This is the one that creates the "Finance gave me that number" excuse. Budget owners participate in the process. They submit templates, attend meetings, negotiate a bit. But they never formally say "I accept this target."

Without that moment of explicit commitment, the budget is just Finance's opinion about what should happen. And opinions are easy to dismiss when reality gets hard.

---

## What Sign-Off Actually Does

The fix sounds simple: make people sign for their numbers. An actual acknowledgment—email, document, whatever—that says "I commit to this budget."

Some budget owners will push back. "We already agreed in the meeting." "This is just paperwork."

But something shifts when you require written commitment. People read the numbers more carefully before signing. They push back earlier, during the process, instead of after the year starts. They ask questions: "What assumptions are behind this?" "Do I have authority to reallocate within this envelope?"

The sign-off doesn't just document commitment. It creates it.

And when someone overspends? The conversation changes. It's not "Finance gave me that number." It's "You signed for this. What happened?"

---

## Why This Doesn't Get Fixed

If sign-off is so obvious, why don't most companies do it properly?

Because it's not a process problem. It's a power problem.

Sign-off only works if there are consequences for missing a signed commitment. Consequences require enforcement. Enforcement requires the CEO to back Finance when a business leader pushes back.

In most companies, that backing isn't there. Finance can ask for sign-off. But when the VP of Sales misses their number and says "market conditions changed," does the CEO hold them accountable to what they signed? Or does everyone just move on?

Finance can build the process. Only the CEO can make it stick.

That's the hard truth. Budget accountability isn't a Finance initiative. It's a leadership decision. Until that decision is made—explicitly, with the CEO's backing—sign-off is just paperwork with extra steps.

This is why so many finance leaders know exactly what's broken but haven't fixed it. The fix requires political capital that Finance may not have. It requires the CEO to want accountability, not just talk about it.

---

## The Revenue Trap

On the cost side, the problem is overspending without ownership. On the revenue side, it's the opposite: targets that are set low on purpose.

You know the pattern. Sales leadership negotiates the target down during planning. They raise every possible risk, argue for conservative assumptions, push for a number they're confident they can beat. Then they hit 102% of plan, declare victory, and collect their bonus.

Meanwhile, the company planned around that conservative number. Investments were sized to it. Hiring was paced to it. The board set expectations based on it.

Written commitment doesn't eliminate this. But it changes the dynamic. It moves from "Finance vs. Sales" negotiation to "What are you willing to put your name on?" That's a different conversation—one where credibility is on the line, not just spreadsheet cells.

---

## Where to Start

The real question isn't whether you have a sign-off process. It's whether you have the organizational backing to make it mean something.

That's a harder problem than process design. It requires a conversation with the CEO about what accountability actually looks like. It requires agreement that signed commitments will be referenced when results come in—not just filed away.

If that backing exists, the process is straightforward. If it doesn't, no amount of process will fix the "Finance gave me that number" problem.

Knowing where you stand—which practices are solid, which are theater, and where the real gaps are—is the first step.
