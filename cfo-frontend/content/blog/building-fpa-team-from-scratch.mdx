# Starting from Zero

You've been hired as the first finance leader at a growing company, or maybe you've been asked to establish a formal FP&A function where one doesn't exist. Either way, you're facing a blank canvas—and the pressure to deliver results quickly.

This guide shares a practical approach based on what works in practice, not what looks good in textbooks.

## First 30 Days: Understand Before You Act

Resist the urge to immediately start building processes and hiring people. Your first priority is understanding the business and its needs.

### Key Questions to Answer

- What decisions does leadership need FP&A to support?
- What's the current pain around financial information?
- What financial processes exist today, even if informal?
- What tools and data are available?
- What's the organizational culture around planning and accountability?

<Callout type="insight">
The most common mistake new FP&A leaders make is building what they've always built, rather than what this specific organization needs.
</Callout>

### Stakeholder Mapping

Identify your key stakeholders and their specific needs:

| Stakeholder | Primary Needs |
|-------------|---------------|
| CEO | Strategic visibility, board materials, scenario analysis |
| Sales Leadership | Pipeline analysis, territory planning, compensation modeling |
| Operations | Capacity planning, unit economics, operational metrics |
| Board | High-level performance tracking, benchmark comparisons |

## Days 30-60: Define Your Mandate

Based on your discovery work, articulate a clear mandate for the FP&A function. This should answer:

1. **What will FP&A own?** (Budget process, forecasting, board reporting, etc.)
2. **What will FP&A support?** (Strategic planning, M&A analysis, pricing decisions)
3. **What is explicitly not FP&A's role?** (To avoid scope creep and set expectations)

Get explicit buy-in from the CEO and CFO (if you're not the CFO) on this mandate.

## Days 60-90: Build the Foundation

### Quick Wins First

Identify 2-3 improvements you can deliver quickly with existing resources. Examples:

- A simple monthly dashboard that didn't exist before
- Automating a manual reporting process
- Creating a forecast template that consolidates scattered spreadsheets

These early wins build credibility and buy time for longer-term initiatives.

### Process Before Tools

Don't rush to implement new systems. Establish basic processes first:

- **Monthly close calendar**: Who delivers what, when
- **Forecast rhythm**: Cadence, participants, deliverables
- **Reporting standards**: Consistent definitions, formats, distribution

Once processes are stable, you'll make much better technology decisions.

<KeyTakeaway>
A good process on a mediocre tool beats a mediocre process on a great tool, every time.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Hiring Your First Analysts

### When to Hire

Add headcount when you can clearly articulate what the person will do and you're confident you can keep them fully utilized. Hiring too early creates problems—people without clear mandates create work to justify their existence.

### What to Look For

For your first 1-2 hires, prioritize:

1. **Business acumen over technical skills**: You can teach Excel; you can't easily teach business judgment
2. **Intellectual curiosity**: The best FP&A analysts want to understand why, not just what
3. **Communication skills**: Analysis that can't be communicated has no value
4. **Adaptability**: In a new function, priorities will shift constantly

### Structuring the Team

Start with generalists who can handle multiple areas. Specialize only as scale demands it. A common early structure:

- **You**: Strategy, board materials, executive partnership
- **Analyst 1**: Revenue-side analysis, sales support
- **Analyst 2**: Cost analysis, operational support

## Common Pitfalls

**Building for scale too early**: Don't implement enterprise-grade tools and processes for a team of three. Keep things simple until complexity is truly warranted.

**Over-promising accuracy**: Early forecasts will be wrong. Set expectations that precision will improve over time as you learn the business.

**Isolation from the business**: FP&A that operates in a finance silo fails. Force yourself and your team into business meetings and operational discussions.

---

Building an FP&A function from scratch is both challenging and rewarding. You have the rare opportunity to design something purpose-built for your organization's needs, without the constraints of legacy processes and organizational baggage. Use that freedom wisely.
