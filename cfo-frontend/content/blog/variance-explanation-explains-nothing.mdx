# The Number You Can't Explain

The CEO asks why you missed revenue by €2M.

You have an answer. Your team prepared it. "Timing on two large deals that slipped to Q2. Lower volume in the mid-market segment. Some FX headwind."

It sounds right. The math adds up. But as you're saying it, a question flickers in the back of your mind: Is this actually what happened? Or is this just the story we constructed from the variance report?

You've been here before. The explanation that's technically accurate but somehow doesn't feel true. The post-mortem that assigns causes without anyone really knowing. The confidence in your voice that's slightly performed.

This is what happens when variance analysis becomes theater. You stop knowing what's real.

---

## The Report You Don't Trust

Every month, your team produces a variance report. Budget vs. actuals. Commentary on the big moves. It lands in your inbox, gets reviewed in a meeting, and then... what?

Be honest: do you trust it?

Not whether the numbers are accurate. Whether the explanations are real. Whether "timing" actually means timing, or whether it means "I don't know and this is easier than finding out." Whether the action items from last month were done, or just quietly dropped.

Most CFOs and VP Finance leaders have a complicated relationship with their variance process. They know it exists. They know it's important. They also know, somewhere they don't talk about, that it's not telling them what they need to know.

The report becomes a ritual. Finance produces it to show they did their job. Business reviews it to show they're engaged. Everyone performs their role. But the actual question —why did this happen and what are we doing about it?— goes unanswered.

---

## What You're Actually Missing

When variance analysis is working, you know your business. Not just the numbers—the dynamics. You can feel when something's off before it shows up in the P&L. You know which explanations to trust and which to probe.

When it's theater, you're flying blind with a dashboard that looks like it works.

The danger isn't bad reports. It's false confidence. You think you know why revenue missed, but you're actually just repeating what someone wrote in a commentary box. You think cost is under control because the variance is "favorable," but you don't know if that's efficiency or just unfilled headcount.

The board asks a question. You give an answer. But you don't actually *know*—you're just passing along the story your team constructed. And if that story is wrong, you won't find out until it's too late to do anything about it.

This is the cost that doesn't show up in the variance report: the erosion of your own understanding of the business.

---

## The Standard You're Setting

Here's the uncomfortable part.

The variance process is theater because you let it be theater.

Every time you accept "timing" without asking "show me the offset," you're training your team that "timing" is an acceptable answer. Every time a weak explanation goes unchallenged, the standard drops. Every time last month's action items aren't reviewed, the message is clear: follow-through doesn't matter.

Your team is watching. They're learning what you actually care about versus what you say you care about. And if variance analysis is just a box to check, they'll treat it that way. Your best analysts—the ones who could actually find insights—learn that insights don't matter here. They stop digging. They start copying last month's commentary with updated numbers.

Eventually, the good ones leave for somewhere that takes analysis seriously.

The variance process reflects leadership's real priorities, not stated priorities. If it's broken, that's a signal about what's been tolerated.

---

## The Conversation That Changes It

Fixing this isn't about better templates or new software. It's about raising the standard—and being willing to hold it.

That means pushing back when an explanation doesn't make sense. Not accepting "timing" without evidence of an offset. Asking "what are we doing about this?" and expecting a real answer with an owner and a deadline.

It means opening next month's review by asking: "What happened with the actions we agreed to last month?" And not moving on until there's a real answer.

This is uncomfortable. Business leaders don't like being challenged. They especially don't like being held accountable for things they said they'd do. Some of them will push back. Some will complain that Finance is being difficult.

That's when it matters whether you hold the line or let it slide.

The CFOs and VP Finance leaders who have variance processes that actually work didn't get there with better process documentation. They got there by making it clear—through their own behavior in meetings—that weak explanations and dropped commitments aren't acceptable.

---

## What You're Really Deciding

This isn't about variance analysis. It's about whether you want to know what's actually happening in your business.

If variance review is just a ritual, that's a choice. It's a choice to operate with less visibility than you could have. A choice to rely on stories instead of understanding. A choice to let your team's analytical capability atrophy because you never demand they use it.

Some leaders make that choice consciously—they have other priorities, other ways of staying close to the business. Fair enough.

But most leaders don't make it consciously. They inherit a broken process, tolerate it because fixing it is hard, and slowly lose their feel for what's real. By the time they realize it, they're explaining a miss to the board with words they don't fully believe.

The question isn't whether your variance process has the right mechanics. It's whether you're getting what you need from it. And if you're not, what you're willing to do about it.
