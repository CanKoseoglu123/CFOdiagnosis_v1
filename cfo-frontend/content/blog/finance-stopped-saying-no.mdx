# The Deal You Knew Would Fail

There's a deal you remember. Maybe it was a big contract with bad terms. A product launch that didn't make financial sense. An acquisition that everyone wanted but the numbers didn't support.

Finance flagged it. You raised concerns in the meeting. You showed the analysis—the thin margins, the optimistic assumptions, the risks that weren't priced in.

And then it closed anyway.

Six months later, it played out exactly how you predicted. The margin eroded. The assumptions didn't hold. Someone asked "how did we let this happen?" and the answer was: you didn't let it happen. You warned them. They just didn't listen.

You've been here before. The pattern is familiar: Finance raises concerns, business acknowledges concerns, deal proceeds unchanged. Your analysis was right. It just didn't matter.

---

## The Seat That Isn't Influence

You have a seat at the table. The CFO is on the leadership team. Finance is consulted before major decisions. There's a review process for deals above a certain threshold.

But consultation isn't influence.

Influence is when Finance changes outcomes. When a deal gets restructured because the numbers don't work. When a project gets killed because the ROI isn't there. When a business leader hears Finance's concerns and actually changes course.

When was the last time that happened?

If you can't point to a specific deal that Finance stopped or materially changed in the last twelve months, something is off. Maybe it's willingness. Maybe it's leverage. Maybe it's the structure you're operating in. But something isn't working.

---

## The Slow Retreat

Most CFOs and VP Finance leaders started their careers willing to push back. To challenge assumptions. To say "this doesn't work" when it didn't work.

Somewhere along the way, many stopped.

Not consciously. It happens gradually. You raise concerns on a deal and get overruled. You flag risks and watch them get ignored. You learn which battles are worth fighting and which are just going to create friction without changing anything.

You pick your spots. Then you pick fewer spots. Then you stop picking spots at all.

The business learns too. They learn that Finance will "raise concerns" but won't actually block anything. That the review process is a hurdle to clear, not a real gate. That if they push back hard enough, Finance will eventually sign off.

Once that expectation sets in, you're not a strategic partner. You're a compliance function with a nicer title.

---

## The Leverage Question

Here's where it gets complicated.

Influence isn't just about willingness to fight. It's about having the position to make your fight matter.

Some CFOs operate in structures where Finance simply doesn't have the power to stop things. Founder-led companies where the founder decides. PE-backed situations where the deal team has the sponsor's ear. Cultures where "Finance is being difficult" is a career-limiting label.

In those situations, you can do everything right—build credibility, document concerns, pick your battles carefully—and still get overruled every time. Not because you lack courage, but because the structure doesn't give you the leverage.

That's a real constraint. Pretending it doesn't exist isn't helpful.

But here's the question worth sitting with: is the constraint as fixed as it feels? Or has the slow retreat—the years of not pushing back—contributed to where you are now?

Sometimes CFOs have more leverage than they use. They've assumed they can't win, so they stopped trying, and the assumption became self-fulfilling. The organization learned that Finance won't fight, so now Finance can't fight—because nobody expects them to, and nobody backs them when they do.

---

## When Saying No Has Costs

There's another reality the "be a strategic partner" narrative often ignores: saying no has real costs.

CFOs have damaged relationships by pushing too hard. They've been marginalized for being "difficult." Some have lost their jobs—not because they were wrong, but because they created too much friction with the wrong people.

Picking a fight isn't always the right move. Sometimes the politically smart play is to document your concerns, get overruled, and live to fight another day. Sometimes the battle isn't worth the cost, especially if you're going to lose anyway.

This is a calculation, not a courage test. Senior leaders have to weigh the value of pushing back against the cost of the friction it creates—and sometimes the answer is to stand down.

The problem is when standing down becomes the default. When the calculation happens so automatically that it's not really a calculation anymore. When you've stopped asking "should I fight this?" because the answer is always no.

That's the retreat. Not a single decision to stop pushing back, but a thousand small decisions that add up to the same thing.

---

## The Question Only You Can Answer

Every CFO operates in a different context. Different CEO, different board, different culture, different history. What works in one organization would fail in another.

So there's no universal answer to "should you push back more?" It depends on your leverage, your credibility, your relationships, and what you're willing to risk.

But there's a question worth asking yourself honestly:

Given your situation—your organization, your position, your relationships—are you using the leverage you actually have? Or are you holding back because it's easier, telling yourself you can't win fights you've never actually tried to win?

The deal you flagged that failed—what would have happened if you'd refused to approve it? Maybe the CEO would have overruled you anyway. Maybe it wouldn't have changed anything except your relationship with the business leader.

Or maybe it would have changed everything.

You don't know. And that uncertainty is exactly why this is hard. Pushing back has real costs and uncertain benefits. Standing down has certain costs—your influence erodes, your concerns stop mattering—but they're slower and easier to ignore.

That's the calculation every CFO makes, whether they admit it or not. The only question is whether you're making it consciously, or whether the decision has already been made for you by years of small retreats.
