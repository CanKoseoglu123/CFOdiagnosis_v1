# The Question You Can't Answer

The board meeting is in two weeks. The CEO asks: "What does next year look like?"

And Finance says: "We're still working on that."

You've been there. It's October, maybe November. You're supposed to have forward visibility—that's the whole point of FP&A. But the honest answer is: you can see until December, and then the picture goes dark.

---

## The Forecast That Shrinks

Most finance teams say they do rolling forecasts. And technically, they do—they reforecast quarterly, sometimes monthly. They update revenue, refresh costs, adjust headcount timing.

But here's what actually happens.

In January, you can see twelve months ahead. By September, you can see four months. By November, you're staring at a wall. December 31 arrives and visibility drops to zero.

Then comes the scramble. Close the year, build next year's plan, answer board questions—all at the same time. Every year. The same fire drill.

That's not a rolling forecast. That's a forecast that shrinks as the year goes on.

---

## The Business Doesn't Reset on January 1st

Here's the thing: your business doesn't start fresh on January 1st. The customers are the same. The contracts are the same. The problems are the same.

CapEx approved in October delivers returns in March. Hiring decisions in Q3 hit the P&L in Q1. That big contract signed in November? The cash shows up in February.

When your forecast stops at December 31, you're making all these decisions with half the picture. You're committing to things you can't fully model. And when the board asks what next year looks like, you don't have a good answer—because your forecast doesn't go there yet.

A real rolling forecast keeps the horizon constant. In Q3, you can see Q3 of next year. The distance ahead stays the same, no matter where you are in the calendar.

---

## The Bias Nobody Tracks

There's another pattern that shows up in almost every finance team. You compare forecast to actuals every month. But you don't track *who* is wrong in *which direction*.

You know the dynamics. Sales is optimistic—the pipeline always looks better than close rates justify. Operations is conservative—they've been burned before. Finance adds it up and calls it a forecast.

But here's the problem: these biases repeat every single cycle. Sales is always 15% high. Operations is always 10% low. And if you're not tracking this, you can't adjust for it.

You're just adding up everyone's errors and hoping they cancel out. Sometimes they do. Often they stack.

The finance teams who actually improve accuracy treat this like a feedback loop. They know the Western region runs high. They know the new product line swings wildly. They've learned where the forecast breaks—and they adjust.

This isn't about blame. It's about learning. A forecast that doesn't learn from its own mistakes is just a ritual.

---

## Why This Doesn't Get Fixed

None of this is news to you. You know your forecast shrinks through the year. You've felt the year-end scramble. You've watched the same biases repeat.

So why doesn't it get fixed?

Because extending the horizon means someone has to own assumptions for next year—and that's a hard conversation. Tracking bias means telling people they've been consistently wrong—and that's uncomfortable. Keeping scenarios fresh takes time nobody has.

Most FP&A teams are already stretched. Running the close, answering ad-hoc requests, building board decks. The forecast process stays frozen wherever it was three years ago, when the company was smaller and things were calmer.

The gap between "we reforecast quarterly" and "true rolling visibility" isn't a calendar problem. It's a capability problem—how the model is built, how the process runs, who owns what.

And you can't close that gap without knowing where you actually stand.

---

## Where to Start

The instinct is to fix this with a longer spreadsheet or new software. Add more columns. Mandate a longer horizon.

But the teams who've actually made this shift say the same thing: start with diagnosis. Which parts of your forecast process are solid? Which are just theater? Where does it actually break down?

You can't build a roadmap without knowing the starting point.
