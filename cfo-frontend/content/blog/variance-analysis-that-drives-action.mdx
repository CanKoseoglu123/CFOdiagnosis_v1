# The Problem with Traditional Variance Analysis

Every month, FP&A teams around the world produce variance reports that explain in painstaking detail why actual results differ from budget. These reports consume enormous effort to produce and even more time to present.

And yet, how often do they actually drive meaningful action?

The uncomfortable truth is that most variance analysis is backward-looking noise. It tells leadership what they already know (results were different from plan) without providing the insight needed to make better decisions going forward.

## A Better Framework

Effective variance analysis answers three questions, in order:

1. **What changed?** (The variance itself)
2. **Why does it matter?** (Business impact and implications)
3. **What should we do?** (Recommended actions)

Most teams stop at question one. The value lies in questions two and three.

<Callout type="insight">
The goal of variance analysis isn't to explain the past—it's to improve the future.
</Callout>

## Categorizing Variances

Not all variances deserve equal attention. Consider this categorization:

### Timing Differences
Revenue recognized in February instead of January doesn't change the business trajectory. Flag these clearly and move on.

### One-Time Items
The legal settlement, the equipment sale, the insurance recovery. Explain once, exclude from run-rate analysis.

### Structural Changes
A new pricing strategy, a reorganization, a changed go-to-market model. These require updated baselines, not variance explanations.

### True Operating Variances
These are the variances that matter—differences that reflect actual changes in business performance or market conditions.

<KeyTakeaway>
Spend 80% of your analytical effort on the 20% of variances that signal meaningful business changes.
</KeyTakeaway>

## The "So What?" Test

Before presenting any variance, ask yourself: "If leadership took no action based on this information, would it matter?"

If the answer is no, either:
- Summarize it briefly in an appendix
- Aggregate it with similar immaterial items
- Don't mention it at all

Your role is to surface signal, not noise.

## From Analysis to Action

The most valuable variance analyses end with clear recommendations:

**Weak**: "Revenue was $2M below plan due to delayed customer implementations."

**Strong**: "Revenue was $2M below plan due to delayed implementations. Based on current pipeline, we expect to recover $1.5M in Q2. However, this pattern suggests we need to revisit our implementation capacity—we're consistently resource-constrained. Recommendation: Approve the two additional implementation consultants in the Q2 plan."

The second version transforms a variance explanation into a business case.

## Building the Capability

This kind of analysis requires deep business understanding that can't be developed from a desk. The most effective FP&A teams:

- Attend operational reviews, not just finance meetings
- Build relationships with business leaders who will share context
- Spend time in the field understanding how the business actually works
- Ask "why" until they reach root causes, not just proximate explanations

---

Variance analysis should be a tool for driving business improvement, not a compliance exercise. The teams that make this transition become true business partners—the ones that get invited to strategy discussions, not just asked to explain the numbers afterward.
